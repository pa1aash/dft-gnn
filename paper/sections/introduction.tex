\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Oxygen vacancies are the commonest native point defect of oxides, and they govern much of what makes these materials useful. In transparent conducting oxides they supply free electrons to a wide-gap insulator, and their formation competes with the compensating defects that limit doping \citep{Lany2007dopability,Zhang2001intrinsic,Ellmer2012past}. In redox catalysis and oxygen storage, as on ceria, the ease of removing lattice oxygen sets how readily a surface exchanges oxygen with its environment \citep{Esch2005electron,Paier2013oxygen}. In solid-oxide electrolytes the vacancies are the mobile species that carry the ionic current \citep{Goodenough2003oxideion,Kilner2014materials}; in resistive-switching memories their drift builds and dissolves conducting filaments \citep{Waser2007nanoionicsbased,Yang2008memristive}; and in electrochromic and reduced oxides they introduce the gap states that colour the material \citep{Granqvist2000electrochromic,Chen2011increasing}.

All of these effects depend on how many vacancies exist. In thermodynamic equilibrium the concentration of a defect follows from its formation energy, which depends on the oxygen chemical potential and, for a charged defect, on the electron chemical potential \citep{Zhang1991chemical,VandeWalle2004firstprinciples,Freysoldt2014firstprinciples}. For the neutral vacancy, the only charge state treated here, the concentration varies as $\exp(-E_\mathrm{f}/k_\mathrm{B}T)$ at fixed oxygen chemical potential, so an error of a few tenths of an electronvolt in the formation energy $E_\mathrm{f}$ changes it by orders of magnitude at the temperatures at which oxides are processed and operated. The neutral formation energy is the quantity that must be known, and known well, to rank oxides for any of these uses.

First-principles calculation provides it as the difference between the total energies of a relaxed supercell with and without the vacancy, corrected for finite-size electrostatics and for the band-gap error of the functional \citep{Freysoldt2014firstprinciples,Lany2008assessment}. Each value requires a large supercell calculation with a hybrid or Hubbard-corrected functional, repeated for every inequivalent oxygen site of every oxide, which is why machine-learned surrogates are attractive: a model trained on computed vacancies could rank oxides and sites that were never computed. Two kinds of surrogate are in use. Descriptor models regress the energy on quantities that a calculation of the pristine host provides, such as the band gap, the oxide formation energy and the orbital character of the conduction-band minimum. Graph networks (graph neural networks) \citep{Xie2018crystal,Chen2019graph}, learned encoders of the local coordination environment of every atom, infer it from the atomic structure alone, and have been applied to site-resolved defect energies of oxides \citep{Witman2023defect}. The database of Kumagai et al.\ \citep{Kumagai2021insights}, which holds neutral and charged oxygen vacancies computed with one workflow for \numFilterCascadeStepsReleaseHosts{} oxides, provides the data for both.

The prior art on neutral oxygen vacancies in this database is specific. Kumagai et al.\ \citep{Kumagai2021insights} trained random forests on descriptors of the pristine host and vacancy site and reported a learning curve, the error as the number of training oxides grows, averaged over a hundred fits on different draws of training oxides, with the data divided by oxide rather than by site. Kiyohara et al.\ \citep{Kiyohara2025machinelearning} trained a graph network on the crystal structure alone, with one model for several charge states, and reported a mean absolute error of \numCZeroOfficialComparisonEVKiyoharaPublished{}~eV at the neutral charge state on a single partition into training, validation and test oxides, without cross-validation and without a learning curve. The serial design, in which an electronic quantity is predicted first and passed to the energy model, has been used by Linton, Singh and Aidhy, who predict the Bader charge and then the vacancy energy in metallic face-centred-cubic high-entropy alloys \citep{Linton2026framework}; that is a precedent in a metal, not a benchmark for oxides. We claim no priority for the structure-only result.

What these studies leave open is a question of training budget. A model given the electronic structure of the host starts from physics that a structure-only network must infer from examples, so with few training oxides it should hold an advantage. As the training set grows the network may recover the relevant electronic information from the structure itself, and the advantage should shrink. At what number of training hosts the explicit density-functional information stops adding accuracy beyond structure, judged against a tolerance of \numCfgStatsDeltaEV{}~eV in the site error, is not known. Nor is the residual improvement that perfect information on the pristine host would still give at the largest budget, which bounds what any better prediction of that information could deliver.

This paper measures both. Using \numFilterCascadeStepsFinalSites{} neutral vacancy sites in \numFilterCascadeStepsFinalHosts{} non-magnetic oxides, we train a structure-only graph network (S) and a network that also receives the density-functional descriptors of the pristine host and site (D), and follow the error of both against the number of training hosts, from \numCfgBudgetsHostsItemOne{} to \numCfgBudgetsMax{}. Every split keeps all sites of one formula on the same side, so no compound is both trained on and tested; the training oxides are redrawn for every resample, and every model receives the same budget of hyperparameter search. Random forests on the descriptors of Kumagai et al.\ and a two-parameter physics floor give the reference levels. The analysis plan, the tolerance and the learning-curve protocol were registered before any network was trained, for the originally registered network (v1). The primary network of this paper (v2) differs from it only in the random starting values of its weights; it was adopted after diagnostics of v1 on test hosts, so every v2 result is labelled post hoc. Three further analyses were registered for v1 and are post hoc on v2: a staged model (P), which predicts the descriptors from the structure and passes them to D; the evaluation of the networks on host geometries relaxed with a machine-learned interatomic potential; and a linear probe of the electronic information held in what the structure-only network has learned. Grouped cross-validation over all hosts and additional resamples are extensions, and every result is labelled where it appears.

The scope is set by the data. The models describe the neutral charge state of ordered crystalline hosts, classified as non-magnetic in the release, and only the domain of deep vacancies that remains after the perturbed-host-state filter removes the entries whose two electrons delocalise into the conduction band. Shallow-donor oxides, chiefly early-transition-metal \dzero{} compounds, are absent from the training data and outside the domain of every model reported here. Section~\ref{sec:data} describes the data and descriptors, Section~\ref{sec:methods} the models, the two networks, the splits, statistics and labels of analysis, Section~\ref{sec:results} the results, and Sections~\ref{sec:discussion} and~\ref{sec:conclusions} their physical interpretation and limits.
