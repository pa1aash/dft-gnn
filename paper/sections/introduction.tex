\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Oxygen vacancies are the commonest native point defect of oxides, and they govern much of what makes these materials useful. In transparent conducting oxides they donate electrons to a wide-gap insulator and compete with the compensating defects that limit doping \citep{Lany2007dopability,Zhang2001intrinsic,Ellmer2012past}. In ceria and other oxygen-storage catalysts the cost of removing lattice oxygen sets how readily the surface exchanges oxygen with its surroundings \citep{Esch2005electron,Paier2013oxygen}. In solid-oxide electrolytes the vacancies carry the ionic current \citep{Goodenough2003oxideion,Kilner2014materials}, in resistive-switching memories their drift builds and dissolves conducting filaments \citep{Waser2007nanoionicsbased,Yang2008memristive}, and in electrochromic and reduced oxides they create the gap states that colour the material \citep{Granqvist2000electrochromic,Chen2011increasing}.

Each of these effects scales with the number of vacancies, and that number is set by the formation energy. In equilibrium the concentration of a defect follows from its formation energy at given oxygen and electron chemical potentials \citep{Zhang1991chemical,VandeWalle2004firstprinciples,Freysoldt2014firstprinciples}. For the neutral vacancy $V_\mathrm{O}^{\times}$ the concentration varies as $\exp(-E_\mathrm{f}/k_\mathrm{B}T)$ at fixed oxygen chemical potential, so an error $\Delta E_\mathrm{f}$ in the formation energy changes it by the factor $\exp(\Delta E_\mathrm{f}/k_\mathrm{B}T)$. At \numNetIllusTemperature{}~K, where $k_\mathrm{B}T = \numNetIllusKT{}$~eV, an error of \numNetIllusDeltaE{}~eV changes the equilibrium concentration by a factor of about \numNetIllusFactor{}. The neutral formation energy is therefore the first number a defect chemist needs in order to rank oxides for any of these applications.

First-principles calculation provides it from the total energies of relaxed supercells with and without the vacancy, corrected for finite-size electrostatics and for the band-gap error of the functional \citep{Freysoldt2014firstprinciples,Lany2008assessment}. Each value needs a supercell of a few hundred atoms, a Hubbard-corrected or hybrid functional and a separate relaxation for every inequivalent oxygen site of every oxide. The database of Kumagai et al.\ \citep{Kumagai2021insights} holds such calculations for \numNetReleaseOxides{} oxides computed with one workflow, and it makes machine-learned surrogates practical: a model trained on computed vacancies can rank oxides and sites that were never computed.

Two families of surrogate exist, and they differ in what they read. Descriptor models regress $E_\mathrm{f}$ on quantities from a DFT calculation of the pristine host: the band gap, the oxide formation energy, the dielectric response, the orbital character of the band edges and the Bader charge of the oxygen to be removed \citep{Deml2014oxide,Deml2015intrinsic,Wexler2021factors}. Kumagai et al.\ trained random forests on such descriptors and followed their error as the number of training oxides grew \citep{Kumagai2021insights}. Graph networks (graph neural networks) \citep{Xie2018crystal,Chen2019graph}, learned encoders of the coordination environment of every atom in a crystal, read the atomic structure alone. Witman et al.\ used them for site-resolved vacancy energies of oxides \citep{Witman2023defect}, and Kiyohara et al.\ reached a mean absolute error of \numNetPublishedMae{}~eV for the neutral oxygen vacancy of the Kumagai database from structure alone \citep{Kiyohara2025machinelearning}. The descriptor route needs one pristine-host DFT calculation for every new oxide; the structure route needs none.

The choice between them is a question of training budget. A model given the electronic structure of the host starts from the physics that controls the vacancy: the band gap and the character of the conduction-band minimum set where the two electrons left behind can go, and the oxide formation energy measures how strongly the lattice binds its oxygen. A structure-only network must infer this from examples, so with few training oxides the descriptors should help. Because every descriptor is a function of the pristine structure, a network trained on enough oxides should recover the part of it that matters, and the advantage should vanish. How many computed oxides that takes, judged against a tolerance that matters chemically, has, to our knowledge, not been measured: the published structure-only and descriptor models were trained at one size or on different splits, and the two routes have not been compared on the same held-out oxides as the training set grows.

Here we measure it. Using \numFilterCascadeStepsFinalSites{} neutral vacancy sites in \numFilterCascadeStepsFinalHosts{} non-magnetic oxides, we train one graph network in two forms: a structure-only network S, which reads the pristine supercell with the vacancy site marked, and a descriptor network D, which receives in addition the \numRfElectronicNFeatures{} pristine-cell electronic descriptors of the database, \numNetDescriptorsHost{} host-level and \numNetDescriptorsSite{} site-level (Section~\ref{sec:data}). We follow the error of both against the number of training oxides, from \numCfgBudgetsHostsItemOne{} to \numCfgBudgetsMax{}, on splits that keep every site of a formula on one side, with the training oxides redrawn for every resample and the same tuning budget for S and D. The descriptor advantage $A$, the error of S minus that of D, is tested against a tolerance of \numNetTolerance{}~eV. Three further measurements test the structure-only route in use: whether the descriptors help to order the sites within one oxide, whether predicting them from structure first, as staged pipelines for vacancies in alloys predict Bader charges before the energy \citep{Linton2026framework}, beats learning the energy directly, and whether host structures relaxed with a machine-learned interatomic potential change the error. Random forests on the descriptors and a two-parameter physics floor set the reference levels, and a screening of named oxides shows the domain of the models in concrete form.
